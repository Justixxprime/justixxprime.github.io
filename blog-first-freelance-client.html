Getting My First Freelance Client in Lagos

By Obioma Chibueze Justice  ·  6 min read

When I finished my frontend training in January 2026, I had a certificate, two practice projects built during the course, and absolutely nothing else. No agency behind me, no network of developer friends passing along work, no portfolio anyone outside my own head had ever heard of. Just a laptop and a plan to figure it out.

Here is what actually worked, not the generic advice you find everywhere, but the specific things that got me my first paying client.

I built before I was asked to

Instead of waiting for a client to hand me a brief, I built things on my own: an e-commerce showcase site, then later a full shoe brand concept with a working cart and checkout, all with no client attached. When I finally started reaching out to people, I was not selling a promise, I was showing a working site they could click through on their own phone right there in the conversation.

I looked close to home first

My first real client work was not a stranger found through cold outreach. It came through people already around me, someone who knew I had just finished training and had a business that badly needed a better web presence. Lagos is full of small and mid size businesses running on outdated WordPress sites or no site at all. I did not need to look far to find the first opportunity, I needed to be visible enough that people already knew what I could now do.

I treated the first project like a portfolio piece, not just a paycheck

The fee on an early project is rarely the point. I put more care into that first real client site than the brief technically required, because I knew it would either become the thing I showed the next ten prospects, or it would not. It became the former, and that mattered more than what I was paid for it.

I kept my day job and built on the side

I did not quit anything to freelance full time on day one. I built client sites in the hours around my other work, which took longer per project but removed the financial pressure that makes people undercharge or panic when a client goes quiet for a week. Slow and sustainable beat fast and desperate.

I said yes to work slightly outside my comfort zone

Some of the sites I have built since, a booking platform, an admin dashboard, a nonprofit site, stretched skills I had only partly practiced in training. I said yes anyway, then learned what I needed to learn while building it. That is how the portfolio grew from two projects to ten in under a year.

None of this was a shortcut. It was closer to just showing up, consistently, with something real to point to every time. That still seems to be the whole strategy.
